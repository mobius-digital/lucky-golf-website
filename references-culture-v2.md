LUCKY GOLF — GOLF-CULTURE REFERENCE

Version 2 · A vocabulary Lucky can pull from.

This isn't a required kit. Not every piece of Lucky copy uses these phrases. This is a vocabulary of golf-culture language — the shorthand, sensory, and situational phrases a golfer would recognize — that Lucky can reach into when the piece calls for it.

Use it when the moment wants scroll-stopper energy: an ad hook, a punchy tooltip, a caption that needs a small punchline, a subject line that wants to sound like a golfer wrote it. Don't force it — if a phrase doesn't fit the moment, don't use it. And never use a phrase that doesn't apply to the product. Wedge language doesn't go on putters. Putter language doesn't go on drivers. The Product Reference Guide names what each club actually does — check it before pulling a line.

Emphasis
Single-word or short tags for product tiles, banners, thumbnails, and small design moments. These work as isolated markers — not as adjectives inside sentences. A "FILTHY" tag on a product tile is different from "the Carver is a filthy wedge" in a paragraph. First one lands; second one reads strange. Lucky mixes three flavors of emphasis, and the mix matters — technical-tactile is the default, pure-vibe for punch, reaction words used sparingly.

Purchase and CTA language
General purchase and CTA vocabulary for button copy, footer copy, cross-sell blocks, cart flows, and category pages. These work as isolated calls-to-action — not as phrases embedded in copy. "Bag it" is a great button; it reads strange as part of a product description. The rule
Pull from this list. Don't paste from it. Adapt phrases to fit the piece — "rips back on the green" and "rips back on command" and "rip it back" are three flavors of the same move. The vocabulary is the raw material; the writer picks the phrasing.

And a final check: does the phrase match the product? A putter doesn't "rip back." A driver doesn't "check up." A wedge doesn't "hold the line" the way a putter does. Golf-culture language earns its spot when it's true to the shot the club actually plays. If it's not, it reads as marketing.

A note on CTAs specifically: product-specific CTAs live in the Product Reference Guide (Go get one, Roll it pure, Fill the gap) and stay locked to their products. Those are recognizable brand markers that shouldn't rotate. The general purchase language in this doc handles everything else: cross-sells, homepage buttons, category pages, cart flows, bundles, moments where the product-specific CTA doesn't fit. Product-specific stays consistent; general rotates.

A note on emphasis specifically: the mix says something about the brand. Leaning too heavy on reaction words ("damn," "that's it") starts sounding like a golf broadcast pod. Leaning too heavy on pure-vibe words ("juicy," "filthy," "unreal") starts sounding like a streetwear drop. Lucky's default is technical-tactile — the "clean," "crisp," "dialed" register. Pure-vibe words earn their spot occasionally, as punch. Reaction words are used sparingly.
