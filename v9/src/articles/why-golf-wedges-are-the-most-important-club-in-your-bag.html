Count your next round honestly. You'll swing the driver maybe fourteen times. Everything from a hundred yards in, putts included, makes up most of the card, and it's the part of the game most of us practice the least.

A good drive leaves you a wedge in. A bad one usually leaves you a wedge out. Either way, the next club in your hands has a lot of loft on it, and how close that shot finishes decides whether you're putting for birdie or scrambling for bogey.

Most of your round happens inside a hundred yards. So should most of your practice.

Four wedges, four jobs

  • Pitching wedge (about 44° to 48°). It usually comes with your irons. Full swings from the fairway and longer chips that need to run.
  • Gap wedge (about 50° to 52°). It fills the hole between your pitching wedge and sand wedge, for the shot that's too long for one and too short for the other.
  • Sand wedge (about 54° to 56°). Built for bunkers, and the go-to for most pitches around the green.
  • Lob wedge (about 58° to 60°). Height in a hurry, for when there's a bunker in the way or a green running away from you.

Start with the loft on your pitching wedge (it's on the spec sheet or stamped on the club), then add wedges in steps of four to six degrees. Do that and every distance inside a full swing has a club that fits it, instead of a half swing you're guessing at.

Where to spend the practice time

Pick your two most-used wedges and learn one number for each: how far a full swing carries. Then learn a half swing with the same two. That's four numbers, and it covers more of your round than any hour on the range with the driver.
