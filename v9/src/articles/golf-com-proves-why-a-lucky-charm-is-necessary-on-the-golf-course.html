In June 2021, Golf.com's Luke Kerr-Dineen wrote about a study golfers are going to like. Lysann Damisch, a psychology researcher at the University of Cologne, ran a series of experiments on whether feeling lucky actually changes how people perform. One of them was on a putting green.

What the study found

People were handed a golf ball and asked to hit putts. Some were told their ball had been lucky so far. The rest were just handed a ball. The group with the "lucky" ball holed more putts.

It wasn't a one-off, either. In another experiment, people who got to keep their own lucky charm did better on the test than people whose charm was taken away. And in a third, people who were told "I'll keep my fingers crossed for you" beat the group that got no well-wishes on a dexterity task.

Call it a placebo if you want. The putt still went in.

Why it works

The researchers put it down to confidence. Believing you've got a bit of luck on your side takes some of the tension out of the moment, and a relaxed stroke is a better stroke. It's strongest when people feel unsure of themselves, which describes just about every golfer over a six-footer to save par.
